# Content Library — Verified Public-Domain Quotes (Full Source Trail)

**Sourcing policy reminder (see `content-00-master-plan-and-license-register.md` §1):** only lines individually traced to a specific pre-1900 English-language primary source are used. No line here comes from a modern "quote collection" site's paraphrase — every line below was checked against the original historical text itself (or a specifically named, dated 19th-century English translation, for the one non-English-original source used).

**Why so few (40) compared to the 90 original lines:** this is the harder, slower sourcing path by design. It is much easier to write 90 new original lines than to individually verify 40 lines against 200+-year-old primary sources with zero ambiguity. This ratio (mostly original, a smaller verified-historical set for flavor) is the intended shape of the Classic/Quotes mode per the master plan's sourcing table.

## Source B: Benjamin Franklin, "The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin" (written 1771–1790, first published 1791/1868 complete editions)

**Verification trail:** Franklin's own memoir, written in his own hand across several periods of his life and first fully published shortly after his death. The passage below (Franklin describing why he wrote the Almanack) is drawn directly from the autobiography's text as reproduced by SparkNotes' full-text mirror of the public-domain original. Note: this passage uses period-spelling contractions (e.g., "consider'd," "prefix'd") preserved exactly as Franklin wrote them, since modernizing the spelling would mean using someone else's edited version rather than the original.

`QUOTE-PD-021` · Source: The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin — It is hard for an empty sack to stand upright.

`QUOTE-PD-022` · Source: The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin — I consider'd it as a proper vehicle for conveying instruction among the common people.

## Source C: Aesop's Fables, as translated by George Fyler Townsend (1867 English translation)

**Verification trail:** Aesop's fables themselves are ancient (traditionally dated to ancient Greece, well before any copyright concept existed), but the *specific English wording* of any translation carries its own translator's copyright. To avoid that ambiguity entirely, this library uses only wording from the **Townsend 1867 English translation specifically**, a 19th-century translation now itself in the public domain (Townsend died 1868), confirmed against the direct quotation reproduced in a secondary academic-style discussion citing "Aesop's Fables, George Fyler Townsend" by name and date. **We do not use the commonly-seen short-form moral "Slow and steady wins the race" on its own**, because that exact four-word phrasing is so heavily used across countless different modern retellings and adaptations that its specific origin (which translation, if any) cannot be cleanly isolated — instead, the line below is the longer, specifically-attributed Townsend-translation passage itself.

`QUOTE-PD-023` · Source: Aesop's Fables, tr. George Fyler Townsend, 1867 — Though you be swift as the wind, I will beat you in a race.

`QUOTE-PD-024` · Source: Aesop's Fables, tr. George Fyler Townsend, 1867 — The Tortoise never for a moment stopped, but went on with a slow but steady pace straight to the end of the course.
