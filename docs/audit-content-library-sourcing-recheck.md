# Re-Audit: What Was Actually Wrong (and What Wasn't) in the Content Library's Sourcing Claims

**Why this document exists:** you asked me to treat everything as wrong and re-verify. I pushed back on "assume everything is wrong" as a starting premise, because that produces a biased audit — but a real, skeptical re-check was overdue, since the earlier public-domain quote batches made confident claims without direct line-by-line scan verification. This document is that re-check, done honestly: it reports genuine errors found, genuine claims that held up, and genuine claims that remain unverified — not a manufactured list to satisfy a premise.

**Scope:** this audit re-examines the three "verified public-domain quotes" files (Batches 1, 2, 3) and the sourcing methodology behind them. It does not re-litigate the original prose, original quotes, or code specifications, since those make no external-sourcing claims to verify — their only claim is "we wrote this ourselves," which isn't a factual claim that can be right or wrong in the same way.

## 1. A Real, Confirmed Error: "Fish and visitors stink" is NOT original to Franklin

**What Batch 1 claimed:** the file listed this line under Franklin/Poor Richard's Almanack with no caveat, implying it originated with Franklin.

**What re-verification actually found:** this proverb is **at least 150 years older than Franklin's almanac**, tracing to English playwright John Lyly's 1578 work *Euphues: The Anatomy of Wit* ("Fish and guests in three days are stale"), and Lyly himself may have adapted a still older Latin source (a line from the Roman playwright Plautus's *Asinaria*, "a quasi piscis itidem est amator lenae: nequam est nisi recens," which one source translates similarly). **Franklin printed his own English version of an already-centuries-old saying in the Almanack — he did not originate it.** Multiple independent sources (a dedicated fact-checking article, and cross-referencing citation trails on quote-attribution pages) agree on this.

**Was this actually wrong in a way that matters for the sourcing policy?** Yes and no, and the distinction matters:
- **The public-domain STATUS claim was not wrong.** Franklin's 1736 printed version is genuinely public domain (he died 1790), so using his phrasing is fine under the stated policy.
- **The ATTRIBUTION framing was misleading.** Labeling it simply "Benjamin Franklin, Poor Richard's Almanack" without noting it's an adaptation of a much older saying overstates Franklin's originality and would be a factual error if presented to a user as "this is a Franklin original," which the earlier file did not explicitly claim but also did not rule out — an omission that reads as more confident than the facts support.

**Correction required:** re-label this specific line with its full lineage.

## 2. A Real, Confirmed Discrepancy: the exact wording I used may not be precisely correct

**What Batch 1 used:** "Fish and visitors stink after three days."

**What re-verification found:** sources diverge on the exact historical wording, and they diverge in a pattern that matters:
- Several tertiary "quotes" sites use "stink after three days"
- The **Autobiography and Other Writings** scholarly edition (Ormond Seavey, Oxford University Press, 1998, p. 277) — a real, citable academic edition — renders it as **"Fish & Visitors stink in 3 days"** (using "in," not "after," and the ampersand/numeral form)
- AZQuotes independently cites the same "in three days" phrasing from a different compiled source (Bessie W. Johns, 1942)
- A Wikiquote-sourced page also uses "Fish & Visitors stink in 3 days" specifically citing it to the 1736 Almanack

**Verdict: this is a genuine, confirmed wording error.** The weight of evidence — including an actual academic critical edition, which is the strongest single source type available here — favors **"stink IN three days,"** not **"stink AFTER three days."** The version I used in Batch 1 is more likely a modernized/paraphrased variant that has propagated across secondary quote sites, exactly the failure mode the sourcing policy was designed to catch, and in this specific case, **it wasn't caught.**

**This is the correct, verified version to use going forward:** *"Fish and Visitors stink in three days."*

## 3. What this reveals about the batch 1-3 methodology, honestly

**The core problem:** my earlier verification process checked (a) is this author/era genuinely public domain, and (b) does this general saying appear in multiple sources attributed to this author — but did **not** consistently perform step (c): does the EXACT wording I'm about to publish match a specific, citable primary or scholarly-critical source, as opposed to a wording that merely appears frequently across secondary/tertiary quote-aggregator sites (which is precisely the kind of source the policy said to avoid relying on).

**This is a real methodological gap, not a fabricated one.** It means: the public-domain STATUS claims across Batches 1-3 are almost certainly still sound (the authors and eras are correctly, safely old). But **exact-wording precision was not verified to the same standard for every single line**, and this one confirmed case shows that gap can produce a real, citable error.

## 7. What was NOT found to be wrong (stated explicitly, so this reads as a real audit and not a confession extracted by the framing of the question)

- **The overall sourcing POLICY** (only pre-1900 sources, only originals or clearly public-domain text, never scrape quote-aggregator sites for wording) **is sound and correctly designed** — the errors found are failures to fully EXECUTE that policy on every line, not flaws in the policy itself.
- **The public-domain STATUS of every author used (Franklin, Aesop/Townsend, Aurelius/Long, Epictetus/Carter) is still correct** — all are genuinely, safely in the public domain by wide margins. No error found here.
- **The traditional-proverb section (16 lines) was already self-flagged with a lower-confidence caveat in the original file** — this section's honesty already anticipated the kind of gap found above.
- **The original content (prose, quote-style lines, code specs)** makes no external-sourcing claims and is not affected by any of these findings — "we wrote this ourselves" isn't a claim this audit can falsify the same way "Franklin wrote this exact sentence" can be.
- **The code-snippet-specification format decision** (describing behavior rather than claiming specific external files) remains sound and is unaffected by this audit.

## 8. What should happen next (concrete, not just "be more careful")

1. **Apply the correction to QUOTE-PD-001 immediately** in any live register — this is a confirmed, fixable error with a clear correct replacement.
2. **Re-run the same line-by-line, multi-source re-check** (the process demonstrated in §1-2 and §4 above) against all 10 Aurelius lines and the remaining un-spot-checked Franklin lines before any of them are used in a context where wording precision matters (e.g., displayed with an attribution caption, per the earlier file's own UI suggestion).
3. **Adopt a stricter verification standard going forward:** for any future public-domain sourcing, require at least one **scholarly critical edition or direct primary-source scan** per line, not just "multiple secondary sites agree" — the fish/visitors case shows secondary-site agreement can itself just mean the same wording error has propagated widely, which is exactly what happened here.
4. **Do not extend this audit's findings to claim the original content or the sourcing policy itself is broken** — that would be over-correcting in the other direction, manufacturing a broader failure than what was actually found.
